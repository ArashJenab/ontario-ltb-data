What Ontario's rental disputes actually cost, and who pays

A ledger of Ontario's Landlord and Tenant Board built only from public records: the Board's own order export and the 2021 Census. It counts what the record contains, says plainly what it leaves out, and reports the findings that cut in both directions.

Read this first. This export covers 2026-01-02 to 2026-05-29: 148 days, not a full year and not all time. It holds 40,844 orders across 37,401 distinct cases, because review and amended orders repeat a case. Annual figures are that window multiplied by 2.466. Ontario publishes one rolling current-year file, so no earlier period exists to compare against.

4. What it costs, per landlord rather than in total

The estimated total at stake across non-payment, other-eviction and breach-of-settlement cases is $123M. On its own that number invites the reading that a class of landlords received a windfall. It is spread across about 12,000 separate landlords, and what it means depends entirely on which kind you are.

Dollar figures are estimates, not a census: they extrapolate a sample of order PDFs as cases x found-rate x mean amount, per category. Orders stating no amount are counted as zero, which understates rather than overstates. Method and sample sizes in sources.

5. How often the same tenant comes back

A common claim on the landlord side is that a small group of tenants cycles through the system. It is testable, and the answer is partly yes and smaller than the claim.

What this does not show. 148 days is too short a window to detect someone who moves once a year, so the "different address" figure is a floor on recurrence across tenancies, not a measurement of it. It is also inflated in the other direction, because matching is on name text and two different people sharing a common name are merged. Both errors are real and they push opposite ways. A longer window would settle it, and the only way to get one is to keep snapshotting this export, which the repository already does on every fetch.

7. Is any of this about income?

This gets asserted confidently in both directions. Joining every postal area's case rate to its census profile settles it, and the answer is mostly no.

How often landlords file is close to unrelated to how rich an area is: rank correlation -0.119 against median household income across 371 postal areas, which is about 1% of the variation between them. Rental disputes are not concentrated in poor postal codes in any strong sense, and a claim in either direction that they are is not supported by this data.

One narrower thing is related to income: whether tenants themselves ever use the Board. Tenant-filed cases per landlord-filed case correlate with median income at 0.292, consistent across three separate census measures. Real, and still small at about 9% of the variation.

8. Who the parties are

First names carry a signal about gender. It is a weak instrument and is reported here only alongside how much of each group it actually resolves.

Why the coverage column is there. The name dictionary resolves Anglo and European given names far better than others, so the misses are not random: communities whose names it does not carry are under-represented in the resolved base. The direction of the landlord finding survives any plausible assumption about the missing third, but the ratios should not be read past one decimal place, and none of this is a statement about any named community.
