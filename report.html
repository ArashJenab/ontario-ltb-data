What Ontario's rental disputes actually cost, and who pays

Built from two public sources: the Landlord and Tenant Board's own order export, and the 2021 Census. Where the export stops, this reads the orders themselves.

The short version. Ontario's rental dispute caseload is ordinary in size by international standards. Almost nothing else about it works the way it is usually described.

  • Filing is not eviction. About a quarter of landlord applications end a tenancy outright. Half of all termination orders say the tenancy ends unless the tenant pays.
  • Most landlords here are not companies. Two thirds are individuals, and most of them own one unit and appear once.
  • The losses land on whoever cannot spread them. The typical individual owner's case is bigger than a corporate one, not smaller.
  • Tenants who bring their own case usually lose it, and almost never have anyone representing them.
  • The record is a biased sample of the thing it gets used to describe.

How many people does this actually affect?

About 1 in 24 Ontario renter households has a case filed against it each year. That is roughly half the United States rate. The scale is unremarkable; what happens inside it is not.

What this means. This is not a system in crisis by volume, and anyone arguing from sheer case counts is arguing weakly. The problems below are about who the system lands on and what it does to them, not about how many cases it hears.

When a landlord files, what actually happens?

48.1% of landlord applications end in a termination order, but 47.0% of those are voidable: the tenancy ends unless the tenant pays a stated sum by a stated date. Net, about a quarter of applications end a tenancy outright.

What this means. The disposition is not in the open-data export, and until this was measured the honest answer to "how often does filing end a tenancy?" was that nobody outside the Board knew. It is now roughly one in four. Any figure that treats filings, or even terminations, as a count of evictions is overstating it, and this is by how much.

Who are these landlords?

Two thirds are individuals, not companies. 9,291 individual owners bring 37.7% of cases, and for 85.8% of them it happens once and never again. 91.1% own a single address.

What this means. "Landlords" is not one interest group, and a policy aimed at the corporate landlord hits nine thousand people who own one unit. The reverse holds too: a policy aimed at protecting small owners does nothing about above-guideline rent increases, which are almost entirely a corporate instrument.

What does a case cost the landlord who brings it?

The median individual owner is owed $7,229 after 4.04 months without rent, which is 33.6% of that unit's annual gross revenue before mortgage, tax or repairs. That is larger than the corporate median, not smaller.

What this means. A dollar figure means nothing without knowing what it is a fraction of. The same loss is a line item to a portfolio owner and a third of the year's revenue to someone with one unit, and it is the second group that brings most of these cases.

And when a tenant files?

43.9% of the cases a tenant brings are dismissed, against 7.1% of a landlord's. Tenants are also represented at 25% of the hearings they themselves bring.

What this means. Both halves of this report point the same way from opposite directions. The individual landlord carries a loss they cannot spread; the tenant walks into a hearing without anyone acting for them and usually loses. Neither is the villain in the other's story. Both are the people least equipped for the system they are in.

Is this record a fair picture of eviction in Ontario?

No. Unpaid rent is 63.4% of what the Board hears but only 8% of the evictions tenants report to Statistics Canada. The most common real reasons barely appear here at all.

What this means. This cuts against both sides and is the most misused fact here. The Board's file understates how often tenants lose housing, and it is not evidence about how common no-fault evictions are, because it barely contains them. Anyone using this dataset as an eviction census, in either direction, is using it wrongly.

Board figures from this export. Tenant-reported figures from Statistics Canada, Canadian Housing Survey 2021. L2 bundles own-use, renovation and conduct applications into one code, so the Board side cannot be split further without reading the orders.

Do the same people keep coming back?

Mostly no. 90% of tenants appear exactly once. The 10% who recur account for 19.0% of cases, and they differ in one specific way: they are taken to the Board for breaching a settlement at 3.44 times the rate.

What this means. There is a real recurring group and it is small. It is not the explanation for the caseload: nine in ten tenants here are having one bad year, not running a strategy. 148 days is also too short a window to catch someone who moves annually, so the cross-address figure is a floor rather than a measurement.

Is any of this about income, or gender?

Income, almost not at all. How often landlords file is close to unrelated to how rich an area is (rank correlation -0.119, about 1% of the variation). Gender shows one consistent pattern and several nulls.

What this means. The gender difference is in who owns rental property and who reaches for the Board, not in who behaves how. Nobody in this data is "crazier" than anybody else, and the income result should stop a common argument in both directions: rental disputes are not concentrated in poor postal codes in any strong sense.

What was tested and found to be nothing?

Reporting only what came out is how an analysis stops being evidence. These were tested and did not.

So what should change?

Three gaps, all cheap to close, all in data the province already holds.

The point. Nothing in this report needed data the province does not already hold, and none of it needed access anyone else lacks. It took a few days with public tools. That a private analysis had to read ten thousand PDFs to establish how often an eviction application ends a tenancy is the finding underneath all the other findings.
